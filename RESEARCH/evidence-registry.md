# Evidence Registry

## Evidence levels
A — Strong / established: replicated evidence or authoritative consensus directly relevant to the claim.
B — Supported but limited: credible experimental or meta-analytic evidence, but limitations prevent strong product-level conclusions.
C — Plausible hypothesis: mechanistically reasonable but insufficiently validated for the intended wake decision.
D — Unknown / requires validation: should not drive a production decision without new evidence.

## Core evidence

### Adult sleep duration
AASM/SRS consensus: adults 18–60 should regularly obtain at least 7 h; individual need varies; more than 9 h can be appropriate in young adults or recovery contexts.
Implication: 7 h is not a target to force. The model needs individualization and safety bounds.
Level: A.
https://www.aasm.org/resources/pdf/adultsleepdurationconsensus.pdf

### Individual vulnerability to sleep restriction
Van Dongen et al.: individuals differ substantially and consistently in vulnerability to chronic sleep restriction.
Implication: population averages cannot determine one user's optimal sleep duration.
Level: A/B.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17118094/

### Chronic sleep restriction and recovery
Repeated restriction can produce cumulative deficits and recovery is not necessarily instantaneous after one or two nights.
Implication: current-night signals must be interpreted with recent history.
Level: A/B.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33274389/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23941878/

### Subjective adaptation versus performance
A dose-response study of 3, 5, 7 and 9 h time in bed found performance deterioration with restriction while subjective sleepiness did not necessarily track the full objective deficit.
Implication: "I feel fine" cannot be the sole validation signal.
Level: A/B.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12603781/

### Sleep regularity
Systematic reviews associate irregular sleep with adverse health outcomes; causal interpretation varies.
Implication: optimize over weeks and penalize unnecessary variability.
Level: B.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41259946/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33054339/

### Sleep cycles
Cycle duration varies; 90 min must not be used as a deterministic alarm formula.
Implication: stage transitions can be context but not a clock rule.
Level: B.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37914631/

### Sleep inertia
Waking from deeper sleep can be associated with greater sleep inertia.
Implication: stage information may be a secondary tie-breaker when confidence is adequate.
Level: B.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12531174/

### Smart-alarm evidence
A 2020 smart-alarm study demonstrated feasibility of simplified sleep-stage prediction in its protocol, but evidence is limited.
Implication: laboratory performance must not be generalized directly to Garmin users.
Level: B/C.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33018943/

### Consumer wearable validity
Meta-analysis of 24 studies / 798 participants found significant differences between consumer wearables and PSG for several sleep parameters. A recent comparative study including Garmin found high sensitivity for sleep detection but lower wake specificity and only low/moderate stage agreement.
Implication: wearable data are estimates with uncertainty.
Level: A/B.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39484805/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40303381/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38557808/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41946254

### HRV and sleep deprivation
A 2025 meta-analysis found autonomic changes including reductions in RMSSD after sleep deprivation.
Implication: HRV can support recovery inference, especially as a within-person deviation, but cannot independently determine wake time.
Level: B.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40895095/

### Light and alertness
A meta-analysis of 29 studies / 1,210 participants found that light characteristics and timing influence alertness.
Implication: morning-light integration is a potential future lever.
Level: B.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35142669/

### Two-process regulation
Homeostatic sleep pressure and circadian drive interact to regulate sleep/wake propensity.
Implication: sleep duration and biological timing should be modeled separately.
Level: A/B.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26762182/

## Open questions
1. Which combination of Garmin-derived signals best predicts easy waking for an individual?
2. Can personalization safely shorten sleep opportunity without degrading next-day cognitive performance or recovery?
3. How much does stage uncertainty reduce the value of stage-aware wake selection?
4. What is the minimum useful personalization history?
5. Can the model detect subjective adaptation despite objective impairment?
6. What is the best fallback when Garmin data are delayed or unavailable?
